Every template in the shop's own colours.

Part of the template platform. Owners shouldn't need a designer's eye. They pick one brand colour, or just upload their logo, and StoreEase offers a few colour schemes that the template's designer has already shaped and that we have checked for readability.

Why "a colour picker for each colour" fails

Giving each colour in a template its own parameter (background, button, headings and so on) looks flexible, but it hands design work to people who didn't sign up for it.

Too many choices

Five independent colours can be combined in endless ways, and almost all of them clash. Most owners will leave the defaults, which then don't match their logo, or pick colours that fight each other.

Unreadable shops

Light yellow text on white or a dark logo on a dark header are easy mistakes to make. Shoppers can't read prices or find the button, and the shop loses orders without knowing why.

Templates lose their design

A template's designer made deliberate choices, such as a calm background or one strong button colour. Free colour fields can undo all of them, so a "premium" template no longer looks premium.

The idea in one line

The template decides where colour goes, the owner's brand decides which colour, and StoreEase makes sure the result is readable.

Colour roles: the same names in every template

Templates never hard-code colours. They use a fixed set of roles, which the engine provides as CSS variables. This is what makes one scheme work in any template.

RoleUsed forRule the generator enforces
bgPage backgroundBody text on it ≥ 4.5:1 (WCAG AA)
surfaceCards, product tiles, panelsText on it ≥ 4.5:1; visibly different from bg
text, mutedHeadings and body; prices, hints, secondary texttext ≥ 4.5:1, muted ≥ 4.5:1 for small text

Recipes: what the template developer writes

A recipe describes a look in terms of the brand colour, not a fixed palette. Each template ships 2–4 of them in template.json. The /parameterize-template skill proposes them from the design, and the developer adjusts.

"colours": {
  "recipes": [
    { "id": "clean", "name": "Clean white",
      "bg": "neutral 99", "surface": "neutral 96",
      "text": "neutral 20", "accent": "brand",
      "head": "bg" },
    { "id": "tint", "name": "Soft brand tint",
      "bg": "brand 97 soft", "surface": "brand 93 soft",
      "text": "brand 22 soft", "accent": "brand",
      "head": "brand 92 soft" },
    { "id": "bold", "name": "Bold header",
      "bg": "neutral 99", "accent": "brand",
      "head": "accent" }
  ],
  "default": "clean"
}

How to read it

  • brand is the owner's colour, nudged lighter or darker only as far as readability needs.
  • brand 97 soft is the same hue at lightness 97 out of 100, with only a little colour: a gentle tint.
  • neutral is a grey with a faint hint of the brand hue, so whites and greys feel part of the brand.
  • head: accent paints the header in the brand colour itself.

The owner never sees this. They see cards named "Clean white", "Soft brand tint" and so on, already filled with their colours.

How the generator works

It runs in the owner's browser, inside the template editor, so suggestions appear instantly. The server repeats the readability check when the design is published.

1 · Read the logo

The logo is shrunk to a small grid of pixels. Transparent pixels and the background are ignored, and similar colours are grouped. The result is the logo's 2–4 main colours, ranked by area and colourfulness. Black, white and grey parts are kept for the logo checks, but aren't offered as brand colours. The dashboard already does a basic version of this for the store's accent colour (logoColours.ts).

2 · Build tones of each colour

For each brand colour, the generator builds a ladder of the same hue from very dark to very light (tones 0–100). This uses Material Color Utilities, the open-source library behind Android's "colours from your wallpaper". Its colour space keeps lightness even across hues, so "tone 40" is equally dark for red and yellow. That keeps the contrast rules dependable.

3 · Fill the recipes and check

Every recipe is filled with every brand colour. Each result goes through the checks in the roles table: text contrast, button contrast, logo visibility. A scheme that fails is first adjusted, by nudging the shade the smallest amount that passes, and dropped only if that doesn't work.

4 · Rank and show

The ranking favours the logo's main colour, then schemes whose button colour stays closest to it, then a variety of recipes. A scheme that needed a big nudge (for example yellow turned to mustard) is marked as such. A logo that would fade into the header drops lower in the list. The top 4–6 are shown as cards with the shop's own products in the preview.

Logos with no colourBlack, white or grey logos have no hue to borrow. Then the cards show neutral recipes plus three safe accent colours (blue, teal and amber), and the owner can pick their own.

Catching bad templates before approval

When a template version is uploaded, the pipeline runs every recipe against 12 sample brand colours. The results appear next to the version in the admin portal, so the admin sees weak spots before any customer does. This is the strip for the template chosen in the demo.

✓ works as is~ works after a big shade change: the shop may not look like its brand✗ can't be made readable: blocks approval
$ npm run check -- clean-store ✓ colours: 3 recipes × 12 sample brands, all readable ~ "Bold header" + yellow: button shade changed a lot (tone 80 → 45) ✓ report attached to the version for the admin

Proposal: a recipe that can't be made readable for some colour blocks approval; "~" results are warnings the admin can accept. Whether the logo stays visible depends on the real logo, so that check happens in the owner's editor.

How others handle it

Nobody in this space leaves owners alone with five free colour pickers. The common answers are schemes instead of single colours, and generating them from one seed colour.

ProductWhat they doWhat we take from it
ShopifyThemes define colour schemes: sets of background, text and button colours. Each section of the shop picks a scheme. Merchants edit the schemes by hand; nothing comes from the logo and contrast is up to them.Schemes, not loose colours. We add the logo and the checks.
SquarespaceThe owner sets a small site palette of about five colours. Squarespace derives light and dark "colour themes" from it, and sections choose a theme, never an individual colour.A few inputs, many derived looks; sections stay consistent.
Android (Material You)One seed colour, taken from the wallpaper, generates a complete scheme of tonal palettes with contrast built in. The engine is open source.The generator itself: Material Color Utilities (TypeScript, Apache 2.0).
Canva, Coolors, Adobe ColorBrand kits store brand colours; generators suggest palettes from a photo or from colour-harmony rules (complementary, analogous colours).Brand colours saved once and reused everywhere; palette from an image.

Sources: Shopify colour schemes · Squarespace style editor guide · Material Color Utilities · The science of colour and design (Material)
